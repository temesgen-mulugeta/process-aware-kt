Most knowledge tracing (KT) systems only predict whether a student will get the next question right. The recent ConceptKT benchmark asks a harder and more useful question: which of 55 math concepts will a student be weak in on their next problem? ~~Trained KT models do this very poorly, and only large~~ **The reported OKT deficiency score is low; several trained baselines have no reported score. Large** language models (LLMs), used directly at prediction time, do ~~reasonably well,~~ **better than OKT,** but they are slow and costly to run. This report tests a middle path. We run a small, low-cost Gemini model once on each past solution, offline, to pull out a few simple signals: the concepts the student seems to have missed, the type of error, the faulty step, and a short summary of what happened. A small LSTM then learns from these past-only signals to predict the student's future weak concepts. On the official MathEDU/ConceptKT split (3,644 train and 404 test interactions from 6 students), averaged over five random seeds, the LLM signals raise deficiency macro-F1 over 13 categories from ~~24.0%~~ **24.3%** (a correctness-only baseline) to ~~28.3%.~~ **27.4%.** That covers about ~~60%~~ **40%** of the **observed** gap to a gold-feature ~~ceiling~~ **reference** of ~~31.1%,~~ **32.1%,** and both precision and recall improve. We block label leakage with **occurrence-specific feature keys and** a runtime check that we test to make sure it actually fires, and the full run repeats offline with ~~the same numbers.~~ **a fixed cache and software environment.** The ~~short~~ answer to the research question **remains exploratory: the gain** is ~~yes: even with very little data,~~ **not statistically significant and does not extend to leave-one-student-out evaluation. The full** LLM ~~signals taken from solution steps help.~~ **feature bundle does not exceed a mask-only baseline.**
